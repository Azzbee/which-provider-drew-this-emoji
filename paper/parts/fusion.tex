\subsection{Combining backbones: averaging, learned fusion and decision models}
\label{sec:fusion}
Our final model combines members of the same architecture by soft voting (\cref{fig:arch}). The DINOv2 branch also tried combining \emph{different} experts, on the logic of \cref{sec:background}: an ensemble helps only if its members make different mistakes, and a self-supervised transformer, a supervised CNN and hand-crafted style descriptors see an image very differently. Adam ran these experiments, evaluated on the branch's 1{,}976-image development split\rep; the code and results are in \nolinkurl{members/adam-zeraiki/}, one folder per method.

\paragraph{Three experts} The experts were the two partially fine-tuned networks of \cref{sec:dino} and a deterministic style descriptor. DINOv2 gives 768 features (class token and mean patch token) and \pct{95.90} on its own. ConvNeXt-Tiny gives 768 pooled features and \pct{95.55}. The style descriptor computes 1{,}460 features from a $64\times64$ RGBA rendering: colour grids and histograms, foreground colour, alpha and transparency statistics, gradients, Laplacian summaries and local binary patterns. It uses no file names or metadata. A histogram gradient-boosting classifier reaches \pct{90.03} on these features and extra-trees \pct{84.72}. The reference combination is the equal average of the two networks' probabilities: \pct{97.12} (1{,}919 of 1{,}976).

\paragraph{Learned fusion} Inspired by TRIBE~v2 \cite{dascoli2026tribe}, which fuses embeddings from pretrained text, audio and video models with a transformer to predict brain activity, the team built a fusion head over the three experts (\cref{fig:fusion}b). Each expert's feature vector is standardised and projected to 64 dimensions (linear layer, LayerNorm, GELU) and becomes one token with an expert-identity embedding. A class token joins them, and one pre-norm transformer layer (four heads, feed-forward width 128, dropout 0.15; \num{226631} trainable parameters) attends across the four tokens. A matched MLP on the concatenated projections (\num{224839} parameters) served as a control. The encoders stayed frozen, either with their public pretrained weights or with the task-tuned weights of \cref{sec:dino}. Each head was trained with three seeds (17, 43, 101), its number of epochs chosen on an inner grouped split of 6{,}322 / 1{,}581 images, then refitted on all 7{,}903 training images, and the three seeds' probabilities were averaged. A replacement had to clear a threshold fixed in advance: eight more correct images than the reference (1{,}927).

\paragraph{Decision models} The last idea was to hand the final decision to a model built for decisions. Julia-1 \cite{julia2026} is an open 144-million-parameter model that picks one answer from a list given a state and a question. It received the 21 class probabilities of the three experts, without pixels or labels, and no fine-tuning. Jev (version 1.14.0) \cite{jev2026} is TypeSafe's hosted decision model, which returns typed answers to a question about a given state. It received the query image, one labelled reference image per vendor and, in its final stage, the three experts' probability distributions. Jev was queried only on the 112 development images where DINOv2 and ConvNeXt disagreed (324 requests, \$0.035 of metered usage). Elsewhere the reference prediction was kept.

\paragraph{What we learned} No candidate beat the plain average, let alone the threshold (\cref{tab:fusion}). The closest, the MLP on task-tuned features, was two images behind. The transformer head was 15 images behind. It fitted its own inner validation split better than the MLP, but those tuned encoders had already seen the inner images during fine-tuning, so that signal was optimistic. Decision models without task training did worse still. Julia-1 chose Google or Messenger for 94\% of the images. Jev corrected 8 of the reference's errors but introduced 37. Bounds computed from its cached answers showed that even perfect answers on the remaining images could not have lifted its configurations above the reference, so the rest of the development and test images were never queried. With three experts of very unequal strength and fewer than 8{,}000 training images, extra combining machinery added variance rather than information. This is the same lesson as the weak-member ensembles of \cref{sec:nowork}: averaging works between comparably strong models, and complexity has to earn its place on held-out data.
